The Human Nervous System & The Effects of Drugs

The nervous system is the body's fast communication network. Psychoactive drugs can change the chemical messages passing between nerve cells—and those changes can influence mood, thinking, movement, breathing and heart function.

Explore the science, compare five major drug categories, and test how changes at a synapse can affect the whole body.

Why does this matter?

A drug does not need to touch every organ directly to affect the body. By changing brain signalling, it can influence the autonomic nervous system, motor pathways and sensory processing.

Chapter 01

Organisation of the Human Nervous System

Two major divisions work as one communication network: the CNS processes information, while the PNS carries messages between the CNS and the rest of the body.

CNS

Central Nervous System

Brain: interprets sensory information and supports thought, memory, emotion, movement and regulation of body functions.

Spinal cord: carries information between the brain and body and coordinates certain reflexes.

PNS

Peripheral Nervous System

Includes nerves and ganglia outside the brain and spinal cord. It connects the CNS to sensory receptors, muscles, glands and organs.

Somatic: sensory information and control of skeletal muscles, including voluntary movement and reflexes.

Autonomic: involuntary regulation such as heart rate, digestion and gland activity.

Autonomic nervous system: two balancing divisions

Sympathetic · “fight or flight”

Supports action during demanding situations. It can increase heart rate and redirect resources towards immediate activity.

Think: alert, mobilise, respond

Parasympathetic · “rest and digest”

Supports digestion, recovery and energy conservation. It generally slows the heart in appropriate contexts.

Think: recover, digest, restore
Why this matters for drugs: substances that change brain activity or autonomic signalling can affect not only thoughts and feelings, but also heart rate, breathing, digestion and movement.
Chapter 02

Nerve Impulses

A neuron receives information, generates electrical signals and passes messages to other cells.

Simplified educational illustration. Myelin segments insulate the axon; the gaps between them are nodes of Ranvier.

Neuron parts

  • Dendrites — receive signals from other cells.
  • Cell body (soma) — contains the nucleus and maintains the cell.
  • Axon — carries action potentials away from the cell body.
  • Myelin sheath — insulates many axons and increases conduction speed.
  • Nodes of Ranvier — gaps where the action potential is regenerated.
  • Axon terminals — release neurotransmitters to communicate with target cells.

How an action potential travels

1
Resting potential
Ion gradients and selective membrane permeability maintain an electrical difference.
2
Depolarisation
Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; sodium enters and the inside becomes more positive.
3
Repolarisation
Na⁺ channels inactivate and K⁺ leaves through potassium channels, returning the voltage towards rest.
4
Propagation
The electrical change triggers the next section of the axon.

Why myelin matters: saltatory conduction

In myelinated axons, the action potential is regenerated at nodes of Ranvier, appearing to “jump” from node to node. This is called saltatory conduction and helps signals travel faster. Damage to myelin can disrupt communication and affect movement, sensation and coordination.

Chapter 03

Synapses & Neurotransmitters

At a chemical synapse, an electrical impulse triggers the release of chemical messengers that cross the synaptic cleft and bind to receptors on a target cell.

Illustration: vesicles release neurotransmitters; molecules cross the cleft and activate matching receptors.

Six steps of synaptic transmission

  1. An action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal.
  2. Voltage-gated calcium channels open; Ca²⁺ enters.
  3. Synaptic vesicles release neurotransmitters.
  4. Neurotransmitters diffuse across the synaptic cleft.
  5. They bind to receptors and change the target cell's activity.
  6. Signals end through reuptake, enzymatic breakdown or diffusion.

Acetylcholine (ACh)

Where it matters: neuromuscular junction, autonomic signalling and brain circuits.

  • Triggers skeletal muscle contraction.
  • Supports communication in parts of the autonomic nervous system.
  • Contributes to attention, learning and memory.

Norepinephrine (noradrenaline)

  • Supports alertness and attention.
  • Contributes to the stress response.
  • Helps regulate mood and autonomic activity.
Drug connection: substances can affect neurotransmitter release, reuptake, receptor activation or other signalling processes. The result depends on the substance, receptor and neural pathway—not one neurotransmitter alone.
Chapter 04 · Main focus

Types of Drugs & Their Effects

Select each category to explore its definition, mechanism, neurotransmitter effects, body changes, examples and health risks. Examples are included for recognition and education—not for use.

Safety first: this is educational information, not advice to take, combine, stop or change any medicine. Prescription medicines should be used as directed by a qualified professional. Mixing sedating substances can be especially dangerous.
⚡
Category 01

Stimulants — increasing central nervous system activity

Stimulants increase activity in parts of the central nervous system (CNS). They can heighten arousal and alertness, but excessive stimulation can place significant strain on the brain and cardiovascular system.

How do they work?

Many stimulants increase signalling involving dopamine and norepinephrine. Depending on the substance, this may involve increasing release, reducing reuptake or changing transporter activity. These messengers contribute to motivation, reward, attention, wakefulness and the stress response.

Not all stimulants share one identical mechanism.

What may happen in the body?

  • Increased heart rate and blood pressure.
  • Increased alertness and wakefulness.
  • Reduced appetite and difficulty sleeping.
  • Restlessness, tremor, anxiety or agitation.
  • Severe toxicity may involve overheating, abnormal heart rhythm or seizures.

Examples & context

Caffeine is a widely used stimulant that mainly blocks adenosine receptors, reducing the feeling of sleepiness. Prescription stimulants may be used for conditions such as ADHD under medical supervision. Cocaine and methamphetamine are examples of powerful stimulants associated with substantial health risks.

Short- and long-term risks

Short term: anxiety, insomnia, raised blood pressure, rapid heartbeat and—in severe cases—hyperthermia, arrhythmia or seizures.

Repeated misuse: may contribute to dependence, sleep disruption, cardiovascular strain and mental health problems. Risk depends on the specific substance and exposure.

Biology link: increased catecholamine signalling can raise arousal and sympathetic activity. This helps explain why effects can be felt in both behaviour (alertness) and physiology (heart rate and blood pressure).
☾
Category 02

Depressants — slowing activity in the CNS

Depressants reduce activity in parts of the central nervous system. Depending on the substance, they may cause calmness, sedation, reduced anxiety, impaired coordination or unconsciousness.

How do they work?

Some depressants, such as benzodiazepines, enhance the effect of GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid), a major inhibitory neurotransmitter. GABA reduces the likelihood that neurons will fire. Other depressants act through different receptor systems, so this mechanism should not be generalised to every substance.

What may happen in the body?

  • Drowsiness and reduced alertness.
  • Slower reaction time and impaired judgement.
  • Poor balance, coordination and memory.
  • Slurred speech or dizziness.
  • At dangerous levels: reduced consciousness and slowed or inadequate breathing.

Examples & context

Benzodiazepines are prescribed for selected conditions such as anxiety or seizures. Barbiturates are older sedative medicines with important safety limitations. Alcohol is also a CNS depressant and can impair judgement, coordination and breathing at high levels.

Short- and long-term risks

Short term: falls, accidents, confusion, memory impairment and respiratory depression in severe cases.

Longer term or repeated misuse: tolerance, dependence and withdrawal may occur with some substances. Combining depressants—including alcohol with sedative medicines—can dangerously suppress breathing.

Biology link: increasing inhibitory signalling can reduce neural excitability. If pathways controlling consciousness and breathing are suppressed too strongly, the consequences can be life-threatening.
♡
Category 03

Antidepressants — therapeutic changes in neurotransmitter signalling

Antidepressants are prescription medicines used to treat depression and sometimes other conditions. They are not a single-mechanism drug class, and they are not intended to produce recreational intoxication. Benefits often develop gradually over several weeks.

How do they work?

  • SSRIs: reduce serotonin reuptake, increasing serotonin signalling at synapses.
  • SNRIs: reduce reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine.
  • Other types: may act on different receptors, enzymes or transporter systems.

Changing reuptake is not the same as instantly “creating happiness”; therapeutic effects involve more complex adaptations in brain networks.

What may happen in the body?

  • Nausea or digestive discomfort.
  • Changes in sleep, alertness or energy.
  • Diarrhoea or other gastrointestinal effects.
  • Changes in weight or sexual function.
  • Side effects differ by medicine and individual.

Examples & clinical context

Fluoxetine and sertraline are examples of SSRIs. Venlafaxine is an example of an SNRI. A clinician selects treatment according to the person's symptoms, history, other medicines and response.

Important safety points

Do not start, stop or change the dose without professional advice. Stopping suddenly can cause discontinuation symptoms. Some antidepressants can interact with other medicines; urgent medical advice is needed if severe or unusual symptoms occur.

Biology link: serotonin and norepinephrine pathways participate in mood, sleep, attention and other functions. Antidepressants modify particular signalling systems; their benefits and side effects vary between individuals.
✧
Category 04

Hallucinogens — altering perception and sensory processing

Hallucinogens are psychoactive substances that can change perception, thought, mood and the interpretation of sensory information. Different substances have different mechanisms and effects; “hallucinogen” is a broad label.

How do they work?

Classic psychedelics, such as LSD and psilocybin, are associated with activity at serotonin receptors, particularly the 5-HT₂A receptor. Other substances sometimes grouped with hallucinogens—such as dissociatives—can act through different systems, including glutamate-related NMDA receptors.

What may happen in the body?

  • Altered visual, auditory or sensory perception.
  • Changes in time perception and sense of surroundings.
  • Intense or rapidly changing emotions.
  • Confusion, panic or anxiety.
  • Some substances can change heart rate and blood pressure.

Examples & context

LSD and psilocybin are classic psychedelics. Ketamine and PCP are dissociative substances and act differently from classic psychedelics. These examples should not be treated as pharmacologically identical.

Health risks

Distorted perception and impaired judgement can increase accident risk. Some people experience severe anxiety or panic. Effects can be unpredictable and depend on the substance, environment and individual. Certain substances may be associated with persistent perceptual or psychological difficulties.

Biology link: perception emerges from coordinated activity across brain networks. Changing receptor signalling can alter how sensory information is processed and interpreted.
◉
Category 05

Inhalants — toxic vapours and gases affecting the brain

Inhalants are volatile substances or gases whose vapours can affect the brain when breathed in. Many are household or industrial products intended for other purposes. Their chemistry and toxicity vary widely.

How do they work?

Many inhalants depress CNS activity. Different chemicals can interfere with neuronal membrane function, ion channels or neurotransmitter systems. Some can reduce oxygen delivery or disrupt the heart's electrical stability. There is no single mechanism for all inhalants.

What may happen in the body?

  • Dizziness, headache and confusion.
  • Impaired coordination, judgement and speech.
  • Nausea or loss of consciousness.
  • Some exposures interfere with oxygen availability.
  • Abnormal heart rhythms and sudden severe complications can occur.

Examples & exposure context

Products containing volatile solvents, aerosol propellants or certain gases may pose inhalation hazards. The products are not safe to inhale for intoxication simply because they are legal or commonly found in homes and workplaces.

Serious health risks

Depending on the chemical and exposure, inhalants can cause brain and peripheral nerve injury and damage to the liver or kidneys. They may also disrupt heart rhythm and oxygen supply. Severe poisoning can be fatal, including after a single exposure.

Biology link: neurons need oxygen and stable cell-membrane function. Toxic vapours can disrupt these essentials, affecting brain signalling, movement, memory and vital organ function.

Chapter 05

Physiological & Physical Effects

The brain and body are connected. A change in neural signalling may affect several body systems at the same time.

♡ Heart & blood pressure

Stimulants may increase heart rate and blood pressure. Depressants may reduce some aspects of autonomic activity, while certain hallucinogens can cause variable cardiovascular changes.

☁ Breathing

Some depressants can suppress the brain's control of breathing. Certain inhalants can interfere with oxygen availability or heart function.

☀ Temperature

Severe stimulant toxicity may cause dangerous overheating. Temperature changes depend on the substance and circumstances.

⌁ Coordination & reaction

Sedation and disrupted motor pathways can slow reactions, impair balance and make precise movement difficult.

✎ Memory & learning

Changes to neural circuits involved in attention and memory can affect learning, recall and judgement.

✧ Perception & mood

Some substances alter sensory processing or emotional responses, changing how a person interprets their surroundings.

Short-term versus long-term risks

Short-term

  • Changes in alertness and reaction time.
  • Altered perception, mood or judgement.
  • Changes in heart rate and blood pressure.
  • Impaired coordination or breathing.
  • Acute poisoning, injury or other medical emergencies.

Long-term

  • Dependence or substance use disorder with some substances.
  • Persistent cognitive or mental health difficulties.
  • Neurological injury from certain exposures.
  • Damage to cardiovascular or other organ systems.
  • Ongoing effects vary by drug, dose, pattern and individual.
When to seek urgent help: severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure, loss of consciousness, suspected poisoning or a dangerously abnormal heartbeat require urgent medical assistance. Do not wait for symptoms to “wear off”.

Why do effects differ between people?

  • Age and general health.
  • The substance's pharmacological properties and amount of exposure.
  • Other medicines or substances present.
  • Individual differences in metabolism and nervous system function.
  • Whether exposure is occasional, repeated or prolonged.
